Police Record Screening 
 
Purpose of screening 
Criminal records screening is one factor used to determine the suitability of an applicant 
who will be working with or providing services to children, youth, or vulnerable adults. 
The intention is to identify criminal records which may present risk to children, youth or 
vulnerable adults. 
 
Procedure 
The applicant is responsible to go to his or her local police detachment to request a 
police record check.  There will likely be a fee charged for this service, with the 
exception of volunteers.  Some law enforcement offices may require a letter requesting 
a criminal record check, completed by: 
• the department in the case of; 
o a family child care home provider; or,  
o an adult over 18 years of age residing in the home of a person licensed as or 
applying to become a family child care home provider; 
• the employer/agency in the case of:  
o a person applying to work in a child care centre; 
o a person wanting to work as an assistant or other employee in a group family 
child care home; or  
o a person wanting to work as an alternate in a licensed family child care home if a 
criminal record check is requested by the provider. 
 
The police service will return the completed police record check to the applicant who 
will then provide the results to the department or agency.  If the law enforcement office 
determines that a finger print check is required, the police record screening is not 
complete until the results of the screening are provided. 
 
The department or agency will review the completed search and may record whether 
there was a criminal record and any relevant convictions or outstanding criminal 
charges. The department or agency shall return the police record search to the applicant 
and shall not make a copy.  The police record search is the property of the applicant. 
 
The department or agency will maintain confidentiality with respect to the police record 
information provided by the applicant.  The department or agency shall only use the 
police record information for the purpose of assessing the applicant’s ability to provide 
services. 
 
If there is a police record 
The presence of a criminal record does not automatically disqualify an applicant. The 
nature of the offence and its relevance to the position being sought are factors which 
will be considered.

Police Record Screening 
 
Example of offences to consider are: 
“Sexual interference; invitation to sexual touching; sexual exploitation; incest; anal 
intercourse; bestiality; order of prohibition; corrupting morals; parent or guardian 
procuring sexual activity; householder permitting sexual activity; corrupting children; 
indecent acts; indecent exhibition; vagrancy; duty of person to provide necessaries; 
abandoning a child; criminal negligence; causing death by criminal negligence; causing 
bodily harm by criminal negligence; manslaughter; infanticide, murder; attempted 
murder; assault; assault with a weapon or causing bodily harm; aggravated assault; 
unlawfully causing bodily harm; sexual assault; sexual assault with a weapon; threats to 
a third party or causing bodily harm; aggravated sexual assault, peace bond or 
recognisance; offences involving drugs; violent offences including alcohol.” 
 
This list is not intended to be exhaustive but provides some possible examples of 
relevant offences, depending on the position being sought.  Additional information 
regarding any of the above offences should be obtained from a local police agency or 
legal counsel. 
 
Other factors 
There are also a number of factors which should be examined in relation to the 
existence of a criminal record: 
• the nature of the offence and its relevance to the safety of children or vulnerable 
adults; 
• the length of time between conviction and the time of the  
 search result and the age of the applicant at the time of the offence; 
• the details of the offence(s), the number of offences and any patterns of offences; 
• any efforts of rehabilitation by the applicant; and 
• employment history of the applicant if applicable. 
 
If an application is rejected due to the existence of a criminal record, the applicant 
should be advised as soon as possible.

Menu Requirements Backgrounder  
 
 saskatchewan.ca 
  
 
The guidelines for meals, snacks and beverages in child care settings are based on Canada’s 
food guide, national guidance on infant feeding* and guidance from the Ministry of Health. The 
guidelines are meant to help support children in child care settings to have adequate 
opportunity to meet their daily nutrient requirements. 
 
Canada's food guide (CFG) website provides healthy eating guidance, houses tools and 
resources and can be accessed here: www.Canada.ca/foodguide. Canada's food guide provides 
recommendations on the variety of foods to eat each day as part of an overall pattern of 
healthy eating and nutritional well-being. Canada's food guide recognizes the importance of 
creating a supportive food environment, and that healthy eating is more than the foods eaten. 
It is also about where, when, why and how foods are eaten. 
 
* Health Canada, Canadian Paediatric Society, Dietitians of Canada, & Breastfeeding Committee for Canada 
(2014) Nutrition for healthy term infants: Recommendations from six to 24  months. 
 
Meal, Snack and Beverage Requirements 
The meal, snack and beverage requirements for child care settings are based on the 
proportions of vegetables and fruits, protein foods and whole grains shown on the CFG plate 
diagram image online. Information about which foods are considered vegetables, fruits, protein 
foods and grain options are found in the individual Canada's food guide links. 
• B reakfast  consists of at least two vegetable or fruit options, one grain** option and one 
protein option (not including milk).

Amounts to Offer   
Tracking the amount of consumed food within each of the food options offered to groups of 
children, as well as tracking the amounts of food left over, can support estimating the quantity 
of that food option to prepare and offer the next time that food option is on the menu.  
 
Children decide on how much, if any of each of the foods offered they will eat. They decide how 
much of the vegetables, fruits, grains, proteins and milk options offered and in what order.  
 
Children are generally able to eat the amounts they need for growth and development when: 
• there is a supportive environment for responsive feeding;  
• they are offered regular meals, snack and beverages; and,  
• they are supported to respond to internal cues of hunger and fullness. 
 
The amount of food a child eats at meals and snacks will vary each day depending on the child’s 
activity level, appetite, food preferences, and growth rate. For example: 
• A two-year-old might typically eat half a banana while a four-year-old might eat a whole 
banana.  
• A child may eat a ½ slice of bread one day and a full slice on another day.  
 
Selecting, Preparing and Offering Foods 
To support growth and development in children and healthy eating habits, when selecting, 
preparing and offering foods:  
• Offer a variety of healthy foods that reflect various colours, shapes, temperatures, textures, 
cultures and traditions.  
• Modify (i.e. cut or mash) foods to suit the developmental abilities of children as they 
progress towards a variety of textures in consultation with parents/guardians. 
• Move towards offering dark green vegetables as often as possible (i.e. daily where feasible) 
and orange vegetables regularly (i.e. a few times per week where feasible). 
• Offer a variety of whole grain products regularly when possible based on availability and 
cultural considerations of the populations served. Limit refined grains as much as possible. 
• As often as possible, offer protein options which have little saturated fat and sodium. 
Include options such as lean cuts of meat and skinless poultry, lower fat dairy products 
(instead of higher fat ones).

• Try to offer one meal or snack based on unprocessed plant-based proteins weekly. This can 
include adding a plant-based protein food to a meal or snack that contains animal-based 
protein food and/or offering a plant-based protein food instead of an animal-based protein 
food.  
• Offering a variety of food sources containing iron daily. These include nuts, seeds, legumes, 
eggs (yolk), fish and shellfish, dark green vegetables, lean red meat, whole grains and whole 
grain foods. 
• Regularly offer foods that contain mostly unsaturated fat. Examples include:

o many vegetable oils, such as olive and canola oils, and spreads made with unsaturated

Introducing Complementary Foods  
For infants around six months of age, breastfeeding and/or formula will still be the primary 
source of nutrition as solid foods are introduced as a complementary source of nutrition. 
Infants progress developmentally as they grow, allowing them to consume more textures.  
Complementary foods should be introduced by modifying and offering a variety of progressive 
textures consistent with the progression of an individual infant’s developmental capabilities. 
Children vary in their developmental capabilities, so the appropriate time to introduce 
complementary foods and new textures should be guided by the child’s readiness and not their 
age, under close supervision and in consultation with the child’s parent/guardian. 
 
Foods and Beverages that Undermine Healthy Eating (Foods to Limit) 
These highly processed foods and beverages add excess sodium, sugars or saturated fat to 
children’s diets. In addition to not being part of a healthy eating pattern, when these foods are 
offered, they may reduce children’s consumption of nutritious foods. 
 
These foods and beverages in this category of foods to limit include: 
• processed meats;  
• deep-fried foods, ready-to-heat packaged dishes and sugary breakfast cereals; 
• cakes, cookies, pastries, ice cream, and frozen desserts;    
• pretzels and flavoured crackers; and, 
• sugary drinks such as fruit juice (including juice labelled as 100% juice), fruit juice 
concentrate, sweetened milks (e.g. chocolate milk), sweetened plant-based beverages.

How do we plan and serve the right amounts of foods at each meal and snack?  
Keep track of the amounts of each of the foods served to groups of children and the amounts of 
the foods left over. Use this information to determine the approximate amounts to prepare and 
serve the next time these menu items are served. This will always be an estimate but over time 
the tracked information will be helpful. 
 
As an example, if you have five children eating together at a table and you serve five cups of 
lentil soup but only about two cups are consumed, you can adjust the amount served to this 
group of children next time. The amounts consumed by all the groups of children can be used 
to adjust the amounts prepared. If lentil soup is a new menu item, the amount children will eat 
will likely increase as they are exposed to it more often.  
 
Since there will always be variation in the total amount of food consumed, the following tips 
can help: 
• Reserve extra food in the kitchen to offer additional helpings as needed. To reduce 
waste created when food remains on children’s plates, guide children to learn how 
much to place on their own plates.  
o With supervision, allow children to serve themselves as developmentally and 
practically feasible to ensure food safety requirements. Since this is a learned 
skill, they may need assistance to improve over time.  
o Help children learn to communicate when they are full and when they are still 
hungry. This way the caregiver knows whether they are finished eating or would 
like more food.  
• Encourage children to start with a small amount of food and ask for more if they are still 
hungry. 
• If a child is finished eating, encourage them to stay at the table and join in conversation 
even if they are not eating.  
 
What is responsive feeding? 
Responsive feeding* means that a parent or caregiver responds in a prompt, emotionally 
supportive, and developmentally appropriate manner to the child's hunger and fullness cues. 
For example, when a child says they are “full” and does not want to finish the remainder of 
food on their plate, the child care provider or parent/guardian will acknowledge that this is 
okay and will not pressure the child to eat more or finish what is on their plate.  
 
*Health Canada, Canadian Paediatric Society, Dietitians of Canada, & Breastfeeding Committee 
for Canada (2014) Nutrition for healthy term infants: Recommendations from six to 24 months. 
 

PAGE 24
2 
How do we make sure that children eat the right amounts of foods and get the nutrients they 
need? For example, drink enough milk for their daily calcium needs? 
When child care providers use a responsive feeding approach, in a supportive feeding 
environment, children are able to eat and drink the appropriate amounts for their needs. Over 
the day/days, their needs will be met. Caregivers support children to do this by: 
• providing a relaxed, pressure-free and distraction-free eating environment; 
• offering regular meals and snacks; 
• encourage self-feeding and self-serving from the food options offered; 
• allowing children to refuse a food or eat as much of the food as they would like, responding 
to children’s cues of hunger and fullness; and,  
• when bottle feeding, caregivers learn to recognize and respond to cues and not on how 
much the infant drinks from the bottle.  
 
What do you do when you know that a child will not eat a certain food? For example, if you 
know that a child does not like broccoli, do you serve them carrots instead? 
Children are more likely to eat a previously refused or new food when: 
• They continue to be exposed to the food. Exposure includes seeing, smelling, or touching 
the food as well as seeing others eat it. A child does not need to eat, try or taste a food to 
be exposed to it. 
• The food is offered with a food which is typically accepted.  
• They are not forced, pressured, punished or threatened to eat a food or a certain amount of 
a food. This includes bribing or rewarding. Examples included: 
o “If you eat this food, then you can eat this food.” 
o “If you do not eat this food, you cannot go to the water table.”  
 
Continue to offer new and previously rejected foods on the menu. Keep track of the quantities 
prepared, offered and eaten by the groups of children to reduce food waste.  
 
The guidelines require two vegetable options at meals. If a child does not eat the broccoli they 
may eat the other offered vegetable. The child will have the opportunity to eat other 
vegetables that day when they are offered. Over time, when a variety of vegetables are offered 
on the menu, children will have the opportunity to meet their nutrient needs and learn to eat a 
variety of vegetables.   
 
When providing two fruit options at breakfast, or two vegetables options at lunch time, can 
we serve as mixed vegetables (e.g. peas and carrots) or fruit salad?   
When providing children with two vegetable or fruit options, it is best to offer each of the 
vegetables and fruits separately. Many children do not prefer mixed foods until they have good 
acceptance of all the foods mixed, and sometimes, they never like certain foods mixed. When 
planning and serving meals and snacks, consider children’s past acceptance and keep in mind 
that children’s preferences change over time.

A nice option to offer a variety of colours, textures and flavours is to slice up strawberries, 
melon and grapes on a serving plate, the children can see and select different pieces. This 
meets the requirements of serving “two fruit options.”  
 
On the menu, how do I identify vegetables, fruits, grains and protein options with mixed 
dishes such as soups, macaroni and cheese.  
On the menu, identify the options by the key ingredients. It will help to refer to the recipe. For 
example, a beef stew which has beef and carrots as key ingredients, can be listed as beef and 
vegetable stew, and may be considered a protein and vegetable option. It is important to 
consider the proportions of vegetables and fruit options to grain option(s) and to protein 
option(s) in mixed dishes. If the stew is half beef and half carrots, this would meet the meal 
requirement for each option. If the stew has very little carrots and is primarily beef, offer two 
vegetable or fruit options in addition to the other options required for the meal.  
 
As explained above, some children may accept a carrot when served separately but not when 
mixed in a food such as a stew. When planning and serving meals and snacks, consider 
children’s past acceptance and keep in mind that children’s preferences change over time. 
 
Can I offer frozen or canned vegetables and fruits?   
Frozen or canned vegetables and fruits can be healthy options and help provide variety when a 
food is not readily available due to the growing season, cost, availability at the local store, or 
lack of storage. 
 
When choosing canned and frozen foods: 
• Choose food without breading or sauces. 
• Choose canned vegetables with little to no added sodium. Drain and rinse canned 
vegetables to lower the sodium content. 
• Choose canned fruit or fruit cups with little to no added sugars and not packed in 
syrups. 
• Use the nutrition facts table on packaged foods to help you compare foods. The per cent 
daily value (% DV) helps you see if a food has a little or a lot of a nutrient. Five per cent 
or less is a little, 15 per cent or more is a lot. Choose foods with sodium, saturated fat 
and sugar content that is less than 15 per cent DV.  
 
Why should I offer a variety of protein options, including plant-based proteins weekly? 
Families tell me that these foods are not eaten regularly at home.  
Eating a variety of protein foods is part of a healthy eating pattern. Plant-based protein foods 
can provide more fibre and less saturated fat than other types of protein foods. This can have a 
positive effect on health, including a lowered risk of cancer, heart disease and type 2 diabetes.

Meat, fish and shellfish and poultry contain a form of iron (heme iron) which is more easily 
absorbed by the body. Eggs (yolk) are also sources of iron, along with many other foods. Iron is 
an important nutrient, especially for young children. 
Plant-based foods are also sources of iron, unsaturated fat and calcium which are all important 
nutrients for the growth and development of children. Examples of foods which are food 
sources of nutrients for children can be found at: https://food-g uide.canada.ca/en/applying-
guidelines/examples-nutritious-foods-iron-calcium-unsaturated-fat/#iron. 
 
When choosing plant-based protein, be sure to look for options that are not highly processed.  
 
Do all grains offered need to be whole grain?  
Canada’s food guide (CFG) recommends whole grains as part of a healthy eating pattern. It is 
recommended to offer a variety of whole grain products regularly as part of the child care 
menu when possible, based on availability and cultural considerations. Limit refined grains as 
much as possible 
 
Which foods and beverages are considered “foods to limit” and can only be offered twice a

The foods and beverages in the category of foods to limit include: 
• processed, deli, and luncheon meats;  
• deep-fried foods, ready-to-heat packaged dishes and sugary breakfast cereals;

• pretzels and flavoured crackers; and, 
• sugary drinks such as fruit juice (including juice labelled as 100% juice), fruit juice

Please clarify the milk requirements and how to incorporate it into the menu.  
Since milk is an important source of calcium and vitamin D in Canada, milk is to be offered twice 
a day at a meal or snack in addition to the protein option. For example: 
• Meal: banana, beans, macaroni noodles, tomato sauce with ground beef and glass of milk. 
• Snack: whole grain muffin, pear slices and glass of milk.

offered instead of milk. 
 
Water is also required to be offered throughout the day and with meals and snacks, even when

Can we include prepared foods such as perogies, canned vegetable soups and packaged foods 
such as prepared rice mixtures? 
Certain dishes, such as perogies and sausage, are common among menus in Saskatchewan. 
These foods are highly processed and should be limited; however, they can be still offered if 
they are identified on the menu as the two times that foods to limit are allowed per week. 
Sausages should be sliced (lengthwise and crosswise) to decrease risk of choking.  
 
Cooking homemade meals allows us to control the amount of sodium, sugar and saturated fats 
in our meals. For example, home-made baked chicken fingers lightly breaded using milk, egg 
and bread crumbs is a nutritious protein option. 
 
Am I able to contact other child care facilities to share recipes?  
Yes! Facilities across the province have developed healthy, cost-effective menus based on 
recipes their children have grown to love. Child care providers are encouraged to share recipes 
that meet requirements and are well received by the children.

HOW TO PREVENT PLANT POISONINGS
•  Keep all house plants out of reach of  
young children.
•  Know the names of all your plants, both 
indoors and outdoors. Label each of your 
plants with the correct name so they can be 
easily identified in case of a poisoning.
•  Berries are particularly attractive. Teach your 
children never to put berries or any part of a 
plant including leaves, flowers, stems, bulbs, 
or seeds into their mouths.
•  Never assume a plant is non-toxic because 
birds or wildlife eat them.
•  Do not rely on cooking to destroy toxic 
chemicals in plants.
•  Teas & infusions made from plants can also 
be a source of poisoning.

POISONING HAS OCCURRED:
SKIN
Some plants can cause itching, blistering, or a rash  
when the plant or it’s sap comes into contact with skin.
First Aid
• Remove any soiled clothing
•  Rinse the skin under running water for 15 minutes,  
then wash gently with soap and water . Rinse again.
• Call PADIS
EYE
If the person rubs their eyes after touching the plant or  
if a splash of plant sap gets in the eyes, the eyes may  
become irritated.
First Aid
•  Rinse the eye for 15 minutes with lukewarm water poured 
from a large glass 2 to 3 inches above the eye.
•  Have the person blink as often as possible while rinsing  
the eye.
• Do not force the eyelids open.
• Call PADIS
PLANTS THAT ARE SWALLOWED 
Choking is the immediate concern when a child places any  
plant part in his/her mouth. A piece of the plant may lodge  
in the child’s throat and block the airway.
First Aid
•  If the child is gagging or choking, finger sweep his/her  
mouth and remove any plant parts. If the child continues  
to choke, call 9-1-1 or your local ambulance.
•  Once the plant is removed, gently wipe mouth area  
with a wet cloth.
•  Check for any irritation, swelling, discoloration,  
or difficulty in swallowing.
•  If the child has no difficulty swallowing, give half a glass  
of water or milk.
• Do not induce vomiting.
•  Do not wait for symptoms to appear; symptoms  
may be delayed.

The Child Care Inclusion Program provides support to families and child care facilities to include children 
with diverse needs in child care programs. 
 
The program includes the following principles 
• Families require child care services that meet their needs. 
• Child care facilities need support to respond to the child care needs of families of children with 
diverse needs. 
• Every child should have the opportunity to be included in a program that is developmentally 
appropriate for that child. 
• Early provision of support services and program planning contributes to the optimum 
development of the child. 
• Children benefit if families, child care professionals, schools and supporting professionals work 
as a team. 
• Parents have the right and the responsibility to be involved in the development of programming 
for their child. 
 
Inclusion funding is available to child care centres and licensed child care homes that are providing child 
care services for a child with diverse needs. 
 
The following grants are available: 
 
Individual Inclusion Grant 
• To assist with the additional cost of supervising a child with diverse needs in an amount not to 
exceed $300 per month. 
 
o Level 1    $200/month 
 Support and consultation needed; minimal or no additional caregiving time; 
additional staff time may be needed for program preparation and to acquire 
knowledge, such as to attend workshops, consult with supporting 
professionals and read resource materials 
o Level 2    $250/month  
 Support and consultation needed and short term or intermittent additional

staff, or reducing group size 
o Level 3    $300/month  
 Support and consultation needed and significant and/or ongoing additional 
staffing needed; additional caregiving time of more than one hour per day or 
five hours per week on average

Setting Full-Time Monthly and Daily Fee Schedules 
New Child Care Centres - April 1, 2026 
 
 
Saskatchewan is committed to making life more affordable for families and supporting child care 
facilities to deliver quality early learning and child care services.  
 
Since April 1, 2023, funding from the Canada-Saskatchewan Canada-Wide Early Learning and Child 
Care Agreement (Agreement) has been invested to reduce parent fees for regulated child care in 
Saskatchewan to $10 a day or $217.50 per month for children who are under six year old.  
 
The Governments of Canada and Saskatchewan have signed a five-year extension to this agreement 
to March 31, 2031. The extension agreement, which came into effect April 1, 2026, allows 
Saskatchewan to provide $10 a day child care for Kindergarten children who turn six on or after 
April 1, 2026 until the end of that school year in June or when they turn seven, whichever comes 
first.  
Child care facilities set their own full-time monthly and daily fee schedules within an allowable 
range. Parents with children under six years of age or are attending Kindergarten pay $217.50 per 
month or $10 per day towards this fee, while the remainder of the fee is provided by the Ministry of 
Education through a Parent Fee Reduction Grant. Please see the Child Care Licensee’s Manual, 
Section 83.2.  
 
There is a cap on the Parent Fee Reduction Grant, which means there is also an upper limit to the 
fee schedules new facilities can set for infant, toddler and preschool spaces. This upper limit 
ensures that child care facilities in the province are provided equitable financial support. 
 
There are no upper fee limits for school-age space fee schedules since these spaces are not eligible 
for the Parent Fee Reduction Grant.  
 
The full-time monthly fee schedule you set is made up of the parent fee of $217.50, which 
parents pay out of pocket, plus the Parent Fee Reduction Grant, which the Ministry of 
Education provides directly to your child care centre. Parents are charged a full-time monthly 
fee when their child is enrolled 10 or more days per month. 
The daily fee schedule you set is made up of the parent fee of $10, which parents pay out of 
pocket, plus the Parent Fee Reduction Grant, which the Ministry of Education provides 
directly to your child care centre. Parents are charged a daily fee when their child is enrolled 
fewer than 10 days per month.

What is a fee schedule limit? 
A fee schedule is a child care facility’s per space rate before the Parent Fee Reduction Grant is 
applied, and includes two components: 
 The parent fee of $217.50 monthly (or $10 per day) that the parent pays directly to the child 
care facility. 
 The Parent Fee Reduction Grant, which the Ministry of Education provides directly to the 
child care facility. 
A fee schedule limit is the maximum per space rate a child care facility is permitted to set for 
children under the age of six or until they complete Kindergarten and are enrolled in an infant, 
toddler, or preschool space. New facilities can set fee schedules at or below, but not above, the fee 
schedule limits.  
 
Why can’t I set any fee I choose? 
Fee schedule limits for new facilities are required in order to make child care funding equitable and 
sustainable in our province. Equitable means that the fees a new facility sets are comparable to the 
fees in current operational facilities. Fee limits ensure that funding for child care is fairly distributed 
across the province.  
 
How were the fee schedule limits determined? 
The Ministry of Education determined fee schedule limits by looking at all of the fee schedules in 
the province for centres in community groupings. The fee limits were calculated from actual fee 
schedules from all of the province’s operational child care centres. 
The fee schedule limits are set at the 80th percentile of current operational spaces. The 80th 
percentile means that 80 per cent of current facilities have fee schedules at or below that level. See 
the illustration below. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
How much can I expect to receive in Parent Fee Reduction Grants? 
The amount of Parent Fee Reduction Grant funding you are eligible to receive is equal to the fee 
schedule you set minus the parent fee up to the number of licensed child care spaces. The portion 
the parent pays you directly is always $217.50 per month for full-time child care, or $10 per day for 
daily child care.

For example, a child care centre operator in the Northern Saskatchewan Administrative District may 
choose to set a monthly infant fee of $945.00. This child care centre operator can expect to receive 
$727.50 from the Ministry of Education for an infant enrolled full-time ($945.00 - $217.50 = 
$727.50). 
 
Why are fee schedule limits different for Regina, Saskatoon, their immediate surrounding 
communities and the Northern Saskatchewan Administration District compared to the rest of the 
province? 
The limits reflect regional differences between current fee schedules in Saskatoon, Regina, 
immediate surrounding areas and the North compared to all other areas of the province.

What is a fee schedule limit? 
A fee schedule is a child care facility’s per space rate before the Parent Fee Reduction Grant is 
applied, and includes two components: 
 The parent fee of $217.50 monthly (or $10 per day) that the parent pays directly to the child 
care facility. 
 The Parent Fee Reduction Grant, which the Ministry of Education provides directly to the 
child care facility. 
 
A fee schedule limit is the maximum per space rate a child care facility is permitted to set for 
children under the age of six on or after April 1, 2026 until the end of that school year in June or 
when they turn seven whichever comes first and are enrolled in infant, toddler, and preschool 
spaces. New facilities can set fee schedules at or below, but not above, the fee schedule limits.  
 
Why can’t I set any fee I choose? 
Fee schedule limits for new facilities are needed in order to make child care funding equitable and 
sustainable in our province. Equitable means that the fees a new facility sets are comparable to the 
fees in current operational facilities. Fee limits ensure that funding for child care is fairly distributed 
across the province.  
 
How were the fee schedule limits determined? 
The Ministry of Education determined fee schedule limits by looking at all of the fee schedules in 
the province for homes in community groupings. The fee limits were calculated from actual fee 
schedules from all of the province’s operational child care homes. 
The fee schedule limits are set at the 80th percentile of current operational spaces. The  
80th percentile means that 80 per cent of current facilities have fee schedules at or below that level. 
See the illustration below. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
How much can I expect to receive in Parent Fee Reduction Grants? 
The amount of Parent Fee Reduction Grant funding you are eligible to receive is equal to the fee 
schedule you set minus the parent fee up to the number of licensed child care spaces. The portion 
the parent pays you directly is always $217.50 per month for full-time child care, or $10 per day for 
daily child care. 
 
For example, a child care home provider in Creighton may choose to set a monthly infant fee of 
$945.00. This child care home provider can expect to receive $727.50 from the Ministry of 
Education for an infant enrolled full-time ($945.00 - $217.50 = $727.50).

Preface 
Overview 
The Ministry of Education supports regulated child care facilities with funding for core operating 
expenses, such as adequate supervision of children and health and safety as outlined in The Child Care 
Regulations, 2015.  
 
The policy framework provides operational guidance to regulated and funded child care facilities to 
support effective and efficient management, financial sustainability, and compliance within the funding 
parameters of the $10 a day child care program. 
This framework clarifies: 
• what services are considered core and supported by government funding; 
• what services may be offered as enhanced or optional; 
• what fees may or may not be charged under existing legislation; and, 
• administrative and operational practices that support efficiency and sustainability. 
 
The Role of the 
Child Care Centre Licensee  
Child care centre licensees, whether sole proprietor or Board of Directors, hold primary responsibility for 
the governance and operation of a child care centre. This includes providing strategic direction, 
exercising financial oversight, and making operational decisions necessary to ensure the organization is 
financially sustainable, compliant with legislation, and responsive to the needs of children, families and 
staff. 
The Government of Saskatchewan’s Early Learning and Child Care: Child Care Centre Board of Directors 
Guiding Document outlines the roles and responsibilities of centre operators, including fiduciary 
responsibilities, policy development, financial accountability, risk management, and oversight of the 
child care operations.   
 
The Role of the Ministry of Education  
The Ministry of Education’s role is to administer and enforce applicable child care legislation, 
regulations, and funding agreements, and to provide policy direction and oversight to support regulated 
child care centres. The ministry does not direct or manage the day-to-day operations of a centre, which 
remains the responsibility of the Board of Directors and its management team. 
 
Child Care 
 Centre Revenue Streams  
Regulated child care centres rely on multiple revenue streams to support daily operations and long-term 
sustainability. Understanding the source, purpose, and conditions attached to each revenue stream is 
essential for accurate budgeting, financial planning, and compliance with legislation and funding 
agreements. Facilities should clearly distinguish between these revenue sources and manage them 
accordingly. 
 

PAGE 74
4 
 
Parent Fees 
Parent fees are charged directly to families for child care services. Parent fees provide predictable 
revenue and must be applied in accordance with legislation and ministry policy. 
Parent fees may also include administrative fees as well as optional fees for enhanced services. These 
optional fees must be clearly communicated and are not mandatory.  
   
Government Funding  
Government funding includes core operating grants and other funding provided by the Ministry of 
Education under the Canada–Saskatchewan Canada-Wide Early Learning and Child Care Agreement 
(Agreement) and The Child Care Regulations, 2015.  
Government funding is intended to support the delivery of core child care services for eligible children 
and must be used for the purposes for which it is provided. Centres are responsible for ensuring that 
expenditures align with funding conditions that may apply and that financial records clearly 
demonstrate appropriate, responsible use of public funds. 
 
Enhanced 
 Optional 
Services  
Enhanced optional services are services that are not part of the government funded core child care 
program. These services may include specialty programming, transportation, optional excursions, or 
extended hours.

Child Care Centre Operations Policy Framework   
Part 1: Fees 
Core Service Fee 
A Core Service Fee is charged by regulated child care centres to fund the cost of delivering core child 
care services in accordance with legislative and funding requirements. For eligible children, the fee is set 
at $10 a day or $217.50 per month and is supplemented with additional government funding.  
Core services include:  
• supervision and care for up to 10 hours per day; 
• staffing to meet required ratios; 
• foundational programming (i.e., Play and Exploration Early Learning Program Guide); 
• meals and snacks;  
• developmentally appropriate equipment and materials supplies; and, 
• administration necessary for the operation of a child care centre. 
Policy 
Regulated child care centres participating in the $10 a day child care funding must: 
• charge no more than the regulated core service fee for children who attend 10 hours a day or 
fewer; and, 
• deliver all legislated and funded core child care services within the core service fee of $10 a day 
for all eligible children. 
 
Enhanced Optional Services Fee 
Enhanced Optional Services fees are charged when optional services are provided to families. These 
services are extra and are not funded through the Core Service Fee. They are offered at the discretion of 
the centre and must be clearly distinguishable from core services.  
Enhanced optional services may include, but are not limited to:  
• specialty programming (i.e. swimming or music lessons); 
• transportation; 
• excursions; 
• personal items such as diapers, sunscreen, bug spray etc.; 
• a reasonable rate for extended hours of care beyond 10 hours per day (within parameters 
identified in Appendix 1 Standard Hours of Care and Extended Hours Fee);  
• enhanced nutrition options beyond regulatory requirements; and, 
• offering additional services for which communities may have demonstrated a need for, such as 
before and after school programs. 
Policy 
Centres may charge fees for Enhanced Optional Services under the following circumstances: 
• Families who opt out must be provided with a reasonable alternative that meets core child care 
requirements (e.g., receive the standard meal or snack instead of an enhanced option). 
• Fees are described and outlined in the Parent Handbook provided to all families and in the 
service agreement. 
• Fees are documented with receipts and follow accepted accounting standards. 

PAGE 76
6 
 
 
Administrative Fees 
Administrative Fees are associated with the administration and management of a child care centre. 
These fees are separate from the daily core service fee or enhanced optional service fee. Administrative 
Fees must be reasonable and directly related to administrative functions.  
Administrative Fees can include but are not limited to registration or enrolment processing fees. 
Policy 
Centres may charge administrative fees  provided that fees are: 
• outlined clearly in written policies with appropriate notice; 
• applied consistently; 
• related directly to administrative functions; 
• approved by the centre’s board of directors; 
• reflected in the Parent Handbook provided to all families; 
• documented with receipts following accepted accounting standards; and,  
• accounted for transparency in the centre’s budget and financial statements. 
 
Annual Fees  
Annual fees may be charged by facilities that operate under a governance model requiring formal 
membership or board participation. These fees relate to governance participation rather than the 
provision of child care services, for example, Cooperative Membership Fees. 
Policy 
Annual or governance-related fees: 
• must be clearly stated in governance bylaw documents; and, 
• must be clearly disclosed to families in the Parent Handbook. 
 
Late Fees  
Late fees, such as late pick up and late payments, are charged when children are picked up after 
operating hours as per the service agreement agreed upon by the parents or when scheduled payments 
are not made on time. Late-pick up fees are different than extended hours fees and are considered 
separately. 
 
Policy 
Facilities may charge late fees provided that fees are: 
• clearly outlined in written policies; 
• communicated in advance; 
• included within the agreement for services; and, 
• applied consistently. 
Fees in Lieu of Volunteer Time and Fundraising 
As applicable to each type of incorporated entity, facilities may offer opportunities for families to 
participate in activities such as fundraising or contributing time to support community-building 
initiatives. In certain cases, facilities may allow families to choose to pay a fee instead of participating.

Policy 
Where a facility sets a volunteer and/or fundraising requirement, the following apply: 
• Families may be required to either take part in identified volunteer or fundraising activities or 
pay a set fee if they choose not to or are unable to participate. 
• Facilities must clearly explain the requirements at the time of enrolment, including what 
activities are available, expected timelines, and the amount of the fee(s) in lieu of volunteering 
or fundraising. 
• If families choose to volunteer and/or fundraise, the facility must provide a clear plan outlining 
the activities, expected contributions (i.e., hours or tasks), and how participation will be 
recorded. 
• Facilities must ensure there are sufficient volunteer and/or fundraising opportunities available 
throughout the year to reasonably allow families to meet participation expectations. 
• A fee in lieu of volunteering or fundraising should reflect the expected level of participation 
(e.g., hours or fundraising targets). 
• Facilities must clearly identify whether any fee in lieu of participation is applied per child or per 
family and apply this approach consistently. 
• Facilities may offer flexible payment options, including installment payments, where a fee in lieu 
of participation is required. 
• Any changes to volunteer and/or fundraising requirements must be communicated to families in 
advance, in accordance with timelines set out in the parent handbook, to allow families time to 
review and ask questions. 
• The volunteering or fundraising requirement must be applied consistently.  
• The fee must not affect a child’s access to care or the quality of services provided. 
 
Non-allowable Fees 
Non-allowable Fees are fees that are prohibited as they undermine the $10 a day child care federal 
provincial agreement provisions or are prohibited by provincial legislation 
Policy 
Facilities shall not charge fees for: 
• core child care services; 
• waitlist or application fees; 
• inclusion or disability-related supports; 
• facility infrastructure, capital, or maintenance costs; 
• mandatory donations; and, 
• routine program supplies or materials. 
It is important that child care centres are upfront with families as to the fees they are charging and the 
parents’/guardians’ obligations related to fee schedules, timing of payments etc. The included appendix 
provides a sample template that programs may wish to post in their facility and include in the parent 
handbook.  This ensures transparency and clearly identifies expectations. These fees should also be 
captured with the agreement for services signed between the child care centre and the family.

 Operational Efficiencies  
Effective operational and administrative practices are necessary to support financial sustainability, 
continued compliance with legislation, and the ongoing delivery of affordable child care services. 
Regulated child care centres must manage their operations within available funding and revenue by 
controlling expenses, aligning operations with funding, and identifying efficiencies within existing 
resources.  
 
Programming 
Programming involves the planning and delivery of daily learning experiences that support children’s 
development and well-being in accordance with licensing requirements and early learning principles. 
Policy 
Facilities are expected to: 
• deliver programming that is grounded in play and exploration as a primary approach to learning. 
Facilities can access play and exploration resources and supporting documents through the 
Ministry of Education’s Early Years Portal; 
• use natural and recycled materials to support creativity and critical thinking while managing 
material costs responsibly; and, 
• regularly seek input from frontline staff on programming practices that reduce waste and 
improve efficiency, such as conserving art supplies and identifying energy-saving practices 
within the facility. 
 
Space Utilization 
Space utilization is the effective use of child care spaces to ensure funded capacity is maximized. 
Policy 
Facilities are expected and encouraged to: 
• ensure spaces are fully utilized; 
• maintain enrolment alignment with funded capacity ; consider cost per child per day and staff to 
child ratio cost, and adjusting enrolment mix or staffing assignments when specific rooms or age 
groups operate at a deficit; 
• manage vacancies; and, 
• align operating hours with demonstrated demand. 
 
Right-Sized Staffing  
The alignment of staffing practices and resources with enrolment, attendance, and legislated ratios. 
Policy 
Facilities must always maintain compliance with legislated ratios and are encouraged to:  
• adjust staffing levels as needed to prevent overstaffing. The Budget Template is a planning and 
monitoring tool that can support staffing and overall financial management; 
• schedule staff based on attendance patterns, with higher coverage during peak times and 
reduced coverage during lower-attendance periods, while maintaining required ratios; and, 

PAGE 79
9 
 
• use attendance information and available scheduling tools to stagger staff shifts to align 
shifts with full-time, part-time, and occasional care needs. 
 
Menu Planning 
Menu planning involves the provision of meals and snacks that meet nutritional requirements while 
managing food-related costs. To support this process, refer to the nutrition guidance outlined in the 
Child Care Licensee’s Manual, including:  
• Appendix 4 – Canada’s Food Guide to Healthy Eating; 
• Appendix 5.1 – Menu Planning Form;  
• Appendix 5.2 – Menu Requirements Backgrounder; and,  
• Appendix 5.3 – Nutrition in Child Care: Questions and Answers. 
 
Policy 
Facilities are encouraged to: 
• review menu plans regularly, at minimum seasonally, to ensure alignment with the Child Care 
Licensee Manual and Canada’s Food Guide; 
• for newly opened centres, menu planning should be completed prior to opening to inform 
budgeting, purchasing, and staffing decisions; 
• implement a rotating cycle menu (e.g., three- or four-week cycles) to simplify planning, support 
bulk purchasing, and improve consistency; 
• identify cost-effective food options that meet nutritional standards such as beans, legumes etc.; 
• update cycle menus periodically to reflect seasonal food availability and introduce variety while 
maintaining familiarity for children; 
• take steps to reduce food waste through portion planning, menu consistency, and appropriate 
food storage practices; 
• participate in bulk-buying cooperatives or joint purchasing arrangements with other child care 
facilities or school divisions, where available; 
• explore partnerships with community organizations, particularly in rural or remote areas, to 
offset transportation or freight costs for food and supplies; and, 
• consider, where operationally appropriate, meal and snack schedules (e.g., snack–lunch–snack) 
that meet nutritional requirements while reducing the need to provide additional meals, i.e., 
breakfast. 
 
Centre Operation and Responsibilities – Fiscal Oversight 
Fiscal responsibility means managing financial resources in a way that supports the ongoing operation 
and sustainability of a child care centre. Child care centre operators are responsible for financial 
oversight and decision-making and are expected to carry out their duties in accordance with the Early 
Learning and Child Care: Child Care Centre Board of Directors Guiding Document. 
Policy 
Child care centre operators are expected to: 
• maintain accurate and complete financial records; 
• use public funding only for its intended purposes; 
• monitor revenues and expenditures on a regular basis; 

PAGE 80
10 
 
• prepare an operating budget when applying for or renewing a licence; demonstrating financial 
viability based on expected revenues and expenses; 
• develop and review annual budgets that account for routine operating costs and foreseeable 
pressures such as repairs, staff replacements, and inflation; 
• review financial information regularly to identify cost pressures and inform decisions related to 
optional services; and, 
• comply with all reporting and audit requirements.

providing leadership, maintaining financial oversight, and making day-to-day decisions necessary to 
ensure the home is financially sustainable, compliant with legislation, and responsive to the needs of 
children and families. 
 
The Role of the Ministry of Education  
The Ministry of Education’s role is to administer and enforce applicable child care legislation, 
regulations, funding agreements, and to provide policy direction and oversight to support regulated 
child care homes. The ministry does not direct or manage the day-to-day operations of a family child 
care home, which remains the responsibility of the home provider as the licensee. 
 
Family Child Care Home 
 Revenue Streams 
Regulated child care homes rely on multiple revenue streams to support daily operations and long-
term sustainability. Understanding the source, purpose, and conditions attached to each revenue 
stream is essential for accurate budgeting, financial planning, and compliance with legislation and 
funding agreements. Home providers should clearly distinguish between these revenue sources 
and manage them accordingly. 
 
Parent Fees 
Parent fees are charged directly to families for child care services. Parent fees provide predictable 
revenue and must be applied in accordance with legislation and ministry policy. 
Parent fees may also include administrative fees and optional fees for enhanced services. These optional 
fees must be clearly communicated and are not mandatory.

Part 1: Fees 
Core Service Fee 
A Core Service Fee is charged by regulated child care home providers to fund the cost of delivering core 
child care services in accordance with legislative and funding requirements. For eligible children, the fee 
is set at $10 a day or $217.50 per month and is supplemented with additional government funding. 
Core services include:  
• supervision and care for up to 10 hours per day; 
• staffing to meet required ratios; 
• foundational programming (i.e., Play and Exploration Early Learning Program Guide); 
• meals and snacks;  
• developmentally appropriate equipment and materials supplies; and, 
• administration necessary for the operation of a family child care home. 
Policy 
Regulated child care homes participating in the $10 a day child care funding must: 
• charge no more than the regulated core service fee for children who attend 10 hours a day or 
less; and, 
• deliver all legislated and funded core child care services within the core service fee of $10 a day 
for all eligible children. 
 
Enhanced Optional Services  
Enhanced Optional Services fees are charged when optional services are provided to families. These 
services are extra and are not funded through the Core Service Fee. They are offered at the discretion of 
the home provider and must be clearly distinguishable from core services.  
Enhanced services may include, but are not limited to:  
• specialty programming; 
• transportation; 
• excursions; 
• personal items such as diapers, sunscreen, bug spray, etc.;  
• a reasonable rate for extended hours of care beyond 10 hours per day (within parameters 
identified in Appendix 1 Standard Hours of Care and Extended Hours Fee); and, 
• enhanced nutrition options where families do not provide an alternative. 
Policy 
Home providers may charge fees for Enhanced Optional Services under the following circumstances:  
• Families who opt out must be provided with a reasonable alternative that meets core child care 
requirements (e.g., receive the standard meal or snack instead of an enhanced option). 
• Fees are described and outlined in the Parent Handbook provided to all families and in the 
service agreement. 
• Fees are documented with receipts and follow accepted accounting standards.

Administrative Fees 
Administrative Fees are associated with the administration and management of a family child care 
home. These fees are separate from the daily core service fee or enhanced optional service fee. 
Administrative Fees must be reasonable and directly related to administrative functions.  
Administrative Fees include, but are not limited to: 
• registration or enrolment processing fees. 
Policy 
Home providers may charge administrative fees, provided that fees are: 
• outlined clearly in written policies with appropriate notice; 
• applied consistently; 
• related directly to administrative function; and, 
• accounted for in financial records. 
 
Late Fees  
Late fees, such as late pick up and late payments, are charged when children are picked up after 
operating hours as per the service agreement agreed upon by the parents, or when scheduled payments 
are not made on time. Late-pick up fees are different than extended hour fees and are considered 
separately. 
Policy 
Home providers may charge late fees provided that fees are: 
• clearly outlined in written policies; 
• communicated in advance; 
• included within the agreement for services; and, 
• applied consistently. 
 
Non-allowable Fees 
Non-allowable Fees are prohibited as they undermine the $10 a day child care federal provincial 
agreement provisions or are prohibited by provincial legislation. 
Policy 
Home providers shall not charge fees for: 
• core child care services; 
• waitlist or application fees; 
• inclusion or disability-related supports; 
• home infrastructure, capital, or maintenance costs; 
• mandatory donations or mandatory fundraising; and, 
• routine program supplies or materials. 
It is important that family child care homes are upfront with families regarding the fees they are 
charging and the parents’/guardians’ obligations related to fee schedules, timing of payments etc. The 
included appendix provides a sample template that programs may wish to post in their facility and 
include in the parent handbook. This ensures transparency and clearly identifies expectations. These 
fees must also be captured with the agreement for services signed between the family child care home 
and the family.

 Operational Efficiencies  
Effective operational and administrative practices are necessary to support financial sustainability, 
continued compliance with legislation, and the ongoing delivery of affordable child care services. 
Regulated family child care homes must manage their operations within available funding and revenue 
by controlling expenses, aligning operations with funding, and identifying efficiencies within existing 
resources.  
 
Programming 
Programming involves the planning and delivery of daily learning experiences that support children’s 
development and well-being in accordance with licensing requirements and early learning principles. 
 
Policy 
Facilities are expected to: 
• deliver programming that is grounded in play and exploration as a primary approach to learning. 
Home providers can access play and exploration resources and supporting documents through 
the Ministry of Education’s Early Years Portal; 
• use natural and recycled materials to support creativity and critical thinking while managing 
material costs responsibly; and, 
• regularly review programming practices to identify opportunities to reduce waste and improve 
efficiency, such as conserving art supplies. 
 
Space Utilization 
Space utilization is the effective use of child care spaces to ensure funded capacity is maximized. 
Policy 
Facilities are expected to: 
• ensure spaces are fully utilized; 
• manage vacancies; and, 
• align operating hours with demonstrated demand. 
 
Right-Sized Staffing  
The alignment of staffing practices and resources with enrolment, attendance, and legislated ratios. 
Policy 
Facilities must always maintain compliance with legislated ratios and are encouraged to:  
• review staffing needs regularly based on enrolment and attendance levels; and. 
• use attendance records to support staffing decisions and scheduling. 
 
Menu Planning 
Menu planning involves the provision of meals and snacks that meet nutritional requirements while 
managing food-related costs. To support this process, refer to the nutrition guidance outlined in the 
Child Care Licensee’s Manual, including:  
• Appendix 4 – Canada’s Food Guide to Healthy Eating;  
• Appendix 5.1 – Menu Planning Form;

8 
 
• Appendix 5.2 – Menu Requirements Backgrounder; and,  
• Appendix 5.3 – Nutrition in Child Care: Questions and Answers. 
 
Policy 
Facilities are encouraged to: 
• review menu plans regularly, at minimum seasonally, to ensure alignment with the Child Care 
Licensee Manual and Canada’s Food Guide; 
• complete menu planning to inform budgeting, purchasing, and staffing decisions; 
• implement a rotating cycle menu (e.g., three- or four-week cycles) to simplify planning, support 
bulk purchasing, and improve consistency; 
• identify cost-effective food options that meet nutritional standards such as beans, legumes etc.; 
• update cycle menus periodically to reflect seasonal food availability and introduce variety while 
maintaining familiarity for children; 
• take steps to reduce food waste through portion planning, menu consistency, and appropriate 
food storage practices; and, 
• consider (where operationally appropriate) meal and snack schedules (e.g., snack–lunch–snack) 
that meet nutritional requirements while reducing the need to provide additional meals, i.e., 
breakfast. 
 
Home Provider Responsibilities – Fiscal Oversight 
Fiscal responsibility means managing financial resources in a way that supports the ongoing operation 
and sustainability of a family child care home. The licensee is responsible for financial oversight and 
decision-making and is expected to carry out their duties in accordance with the Child Care Licensee 
Manual. 
Policy 
Home providers are expected to: 
• maintain accurate and complete financial records; 
• use public funding only for its intended purposes; 
• monitor revenues and expenditures on a regular basis; 
• prepare an operating budget when applying for or renewing a licence; demonstrating financial 
viability based on expected revenues and expenses; 
• develop and review annual budgets that account for routine operating costs and foreseeable 
pressures such as repairs, staff replacements, and inflation; 
• review financial information regularly to identify cost pressures and inform decisions related to 
optional services; and, 
• comply with all reporting and audit requirements.

About this Policy  
Saskatchewan's $10 a day child care program has created real change for families. Since 2021, the 
province has added over 146 per cent more regulated child care spaces and significantly increased the 
number of certified early childhood educators. This progress is possible because of the Canada-
Saskatchewan Canada-Wide Early Learning and Child Care Agreement (the Agreement), a binding 
federal-provincial arrangement that sets the terms under which federal funding is provided. 
 
To keep child care affordable for families, as supported by the affordability principles of the Agreement, 
child care centres and family child care homes (hereafter “facility” or “facilities”) must follow the rules 
around what fees they can and cannot charge families. This policy provides further details on fees within 
the Early Learning and Child Care Operations Policy Framework and should be read alongside The Child 
Care Licensee’s Manual. Together, these documents outline what fees are permitted, how to charge 
them properly, and what happens if non-allowable fees are charged. 
 
What the $10 a Day Fee Already Covers  
The $10 a day fee covers the full cost of core child care services. Facilities and providers cannot charge 
additional fees for any of the following as they are considered core child care services: 
• Supervision and care for up to 10 hours per day. 
• Staffing to meet legislated staff-child ratios. 
• Developmentally appropriate foundational play-based programming (Play and Exploration Early 
Learning Program Guide). 
• Equipment and materials needed to deliver a developmentally appropriate program. 
• Meals and snacks legislated under The Child Care Regulations, 2015 (the “Regulations”). Facilities are 
required to provide meals and snacks that meet the nutritional needs of children six months and 
older. 
• General administration that includes the day-to-day running your facility (record-keeping, reporting). 
Note: The Parent Fee Reduction Grant provides $5 monthly per licensed space to offset the time 
spent reporting and administering enrolment, while the Wage Enhancement Grant funds employer 
EI and CPP contributions plus a three percentage for bookkeeping and payroll costs. You cannot 
charge families for costs already covered by these grants. 
If you are unsure whether a fee is allowed, please email childcare@gov.sk.ca before you charge it.

Administrative Fees  
Administrative fees recover the specific cost of a service. They are separate from core services and 
enhanced optional service fees. The following are examples of additional fees that may be charged to 
families: 
• One Time Enrolment Fee: When a new family enrolls their child, a facility may decide to implement a 
fee to cover the administrative work of processing paperwork and setting up their file.  
• Annual Registration Fee: As this paperwork needs to be reviewed and updated each year, facilities 
may choose to charge an annual registration fee to meet with families to ensure all child and family 
forms are current. 
• Late Payment and Late Pick-Up Fees: When a parent pays late or picks up their child after the 
facility's posted closing time or after the hours on their Agreement for Services. Late pick-up fees are 
not the same as extended hours fee. See Extended Hours of Care Fees Beyond 10 Hours a Day below 
for more information. 
• Annual Membership or Governance Fees: If a facility operates as a cooperative or under a board 
governance model, facilities may charge families an annual membership fee. This fee is about 
governance participation by way of voting. (This is not applicable to Family and Group Family Child 
Care Homes) 
Enhanced Optional 
 Service 
 Fees  
Enhanced optional service fees enable facilities to offer services that are responsive to family 
preferences or operational opportunities while maintaining access to core child care services. Facilities 
can charge families for extras that go beyond the core child care services. The key word is "optional" 
which means families must be able to say no, and when they do, their child must still receive everything 
included in the core child care service at no extra cost. The following fee types are examples that are 
allowed as enhanced optional service fees: 
• Specialty Programming: Optional enrichment activities beyond regular programming like swimming 
lessons, music classes, or a language program. Children who are not enrolled must still receive the 
full regular core child care services at no extra cost. 
• Transportation: Optional transport a facility offers, for example, transportation to and from a school. 
• Field Trips and Excursions: Outings that go beyond your regular programming. If a family opts out, 
the facility must provide supervised care at the facility that day at no extra cost. 
• Personal Items for Individual Children: Facilities may choose to provide personal items such as 
diapers, sunscreen, or bug spray rather than asking parents to bring for their child. Parents could be 
charged a fee if they opt to have the facility supplied items used for their child rather than bringing 
their own. 
• Enhanced Meal or Snacks: Food options that go beyond what the Regulations require, for instance, if 
you already serve a morning snack but also want to offer breakfast, you can charge for the enhanced 
option. Other examples could include providing food items for children with special dietary 
restrictions or allergies or supplying infant formula or baby food for young infants. The regulated 
meal or snack is a core service and must be provided to every child at no extra cost.

Extended Hours of Care Fees Beyond 10 Hours a Day  
Starting July 1, 2026, facilities can choose to charge a fee for care provided beyond the standard 10-hour 
day. Facilities are not required to offer extended hours; this is their choice. If they do, there are policies 
facilities must follow and maximum amounts they cannot exceed. For full details and fee cap tables, visit

Why 
Compliance Matters  
Saskatchewan has an obligation under the Agreement to keep child care affordable. Every regulated 
facility that receives government funding has a role to play in meeting that obligation. When facilities 
charge non-allowable fees, it puts the Agreement at risk for every facility, every family and every child in 
the province.  
 
This policy exists to protect the progress we have made together over the past five years and to make 
sure it continues. The ministry's goal is always to help facilities stay in compliance, but it will act to 
protect families and the sector when necessary, as outlined in Section 18 of The Child Care Act, 2014.

Child care development decisions in Saskatchewan are guided by an objective, data-driven framework to ensure 
investments are targeted, equitable and sustainable. Development is prioritized where evidence demonstrates 
the greatest need and system impact. 
1. Demonstrated Community Need and Demand 
Development is considered in communities showing clear indicators of need, supported by: 
• High utilization rates (sustained operation at or near full capacity). 
• Enrolment pressure and patterns of increased applications for new or expanded facilities. 
• Population growth among families with young children. 
• Demographic shifts, including increased numbers of newcomers, Indigenous families, racialized 
populations and Francophone minority language speakers. 
• Absence or limited availability of regulated child care in the community. 
Where spaces are underutilized, approvals for new spaces—particularly family child care homes—may be paused 
until demand increases. 
2. Equity and Inclusion Considerations 
Priority is given to development that improves access for underserved populations, including: 
• Rural, remote, northern and smaller communities. 
• Indigenous children and families, guided by ongoing collaboration with First Nations and Métis partners. 
• Francophone communities, schools and families. 
• Children with disabilities or developmental vulnerabilities. 
• Communities identiﬁed through vulnerability indicators, school readiness assessments and community 
needs assessments. 
Expansion planning incorporates culturally appropriate, inclusive and accessible service delivery. 
3. Alignment with Population and Labour Market Trends 
Development proposals are assessed against: 
• Current and projected population growth and migration patterns. 
• Labour market activity and workforce participation needs, particularly for parents. 
• Regional economic trends that aﬀect family demand for regulated child care. 
• Alignment with provincial strategies, including the Saskatchewan Growth Strategy, Poverty Reduction 
Strategy, Disability Strategy and Child and Youth Strategy.

4. Infrastructure Readiness and Eﬃciency 
Infrastructure-based development considers: 
• Availability and readiness of suitable facilities or construction sites which may include. 
• Use of standardized child care centre design and construction guidelines to streamline timelines and 
reduce costs. 
5. Existing Investments and System Sustainability 
Development decisions prioritize maintaining and protecting progress already achieved by: 
• Preserving existing licensed spaces and backﬁlling capacity where closures occur. 
• Identifying replacement or adjustment needs in response to localized demand shifts. 
• Avoiding over-expansion in regions with declining or unstable demand. 
6. Non-Proﬁt and Public Delivery Priority 
Consistent with the Canada–Saskatchewan Canada Wide Early Learning and Child Care Agreement: 
• Preference is given to non-proﬁt, public and regulated family-based providers. 
• Development supports governance models that demonstrate transparency, accountability and 
reinvestment into quality. 
• Funding eligibility criteria ensure public dollars support providers capable of maintaining quality 
standards and operational stability. 
7. Regulatory Compliance and Licensing 
Development of additional spaces is contingent on a demonstrated history of regulatory compliance, including: 
• Holds a valid Saskatchewan child care licence in good standing. 
• Has not been subject to provisional, suspended or conditional licensing status within a deﬁned period. 
• Demonstrates a consistent history of regulatory compliance, including timely response to licensing 
requirements. 
• Has no unresolved, serious or repeated compliance actions related to health, safety, staﬃng or program 
quality. 
8. Operational Readiness and Eﬃciency 
Development is prioritized for providers demonstrating operational readiness and eﬃcient use of existing 
capacity, including: 
• Eﬃcient utilization of existing licensed spaces, with enrolment aligned to community demand. 
• Stable staﬃng levels supported by appropriate qualiﬁcations and workforce planning. 
• Eﬀective governance and management practices, including ﬁnancial oversight and accountability 
structures. 
• Administrative and operational systems in place to support expansion, such as enrolment management, 
ﬁnancial controls and regulatory reporting. 
 
9. Quality, Inclusion, and Accessibility 
Development prioritizes quality programming and inclusive, accessible service delivery that supports positive 
outcomes for children and families, including; 
• Maintains quality programming and demonstrates positive child development outcomes. 
• Has the capacity to support inclusive practices, including children with disabilities or additional support 
needs. 
• Supports or provides French language child care where applicable, in alignment with Francophone 
minority language needs.
